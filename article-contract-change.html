Why a simple contract change can take weeks, and how to stop it

When a contract says nothing about how changes are priced or how fast they are handled, every change becomes a negotiation. Four clauses fix most of it.

Most long-term service contracts change many times during their life. New sites are added, volumes move, a service is extended or a requirement is dropped. Each change is usually handled through a Contract Change Note, and in many organisations each one becomes a small negotiation of its own. Weeks pass while the supplier prepares a quote, the buyer challenges it, and both sides wait for approvals.

In one programme our founder worked on before Arteze, changes that had been taking around 25 days to agree were brought down to 5. The suppliers did not become more cooperative overnight. The difference came from writing into the contract the things that every change had previously argued about.

Why changes get stuck

When a contract says nothing about how changes are priced or how quickly they should be handled, each request starts from a blank page. The supplier has to work out a price, often with a margin for uncertainty. The buyer has no benchmark, so it challenges the quote. Neither side has a deadline, so the request waits behind other work. The cost is not just the delay. It is also the management time on both sides, and the work that cannot start until the change is agreed.

What a good change mechanism contains

A contract that handles change well usually sets out four things.

  1. A rate card for change work, so that most changes can be priced from agreed day rates or unit prices rather than negotiated from scratch.
  2. Turnaround times, for example a fixed number of working days for the supplier to provide an impact assessment and a quote, and for the buyer to respond.
  3. Categories of change, so that small, routine changes follow a quick route with lighter approval, while major changes get proper scrutiny.
  4. Clear authority, naming who on each side can approve changes up to what value.

None of this is complicated, but it is much easier to agree at the start of a contract than halfway through it.

If your contract is already signed

You do not have to wait for renewal. Look at the changes raised over the last year, how long each took and how they were priced. That evidence usually makes a strong case for agreeing a change procedure as a variation to the contract, and suppliers often welcome it because slow changes cost them money too.
