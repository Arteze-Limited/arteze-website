Contract exit and transition · 5 min read

Why the best time to plan your exit from a supplier is before you sign

Thin exit clauses are cheap to agree and expensive to live with. Here is what a good exit schedule covers, and what to do if yours is weak.

When a new supplier contract is being negotiated, both sides are optimistic. The relationship is new, the business case is approved and nobody wants to spend time discussing how it might end. So the exit clauses are often thin: a general obligation to cooperate, a vague reference to handing over data, and very little else.

Years later, when the organisation wants to move to a new provider, those thin clauses become expensive. The incumbent has no clear obligation to help, and every piece of support during the transition becomes a chargeable extra. In the worst cases the contract allows heavy disengagement fees, and the buyer finds that leaving costs far more than the business case for the new supplier assumed.

What a supplier owes you at exit depends on what you wrote down

At the point of exit, the incumbent's commercial interest has changed. It is losing the work, so it has little reason to make the handover easy unless the contract requires it. That is not a criticism of suppliers. It is simply why the obligations need to be agreed while you still have the leverage of a new contract on the table.

What good exit terms cover

A well-drafted exit schedule usually includes the following.

  • An exit plan, prepared by the supplier early in the contract and kept up to date, rather than written in a hurry when notice is given.
  • Transition assistance, describing what help the supplier will give the new provider and for how long, and how it will be charged. Ideally this is at pre-agreed rates, not open to negotiation.
  • Data and documentation, setting out what will be returned, in what format and by when, including configuration records and operating procedures.
  • People and knowledge, covering access to key staff during handover and any employment law obligations that may apply.
  • Continuity of service, so that service levels continue to apply until the new provider has taken over.
  • Termination charges, stated clearly and reducing over the term, so that the cost of leaving is known in advance.

Testing the plan

An exit plan that has never been reviewed is unlikely to work when it is needed. For critical suppliers it is worth walking through the plan every year or two: checking that the data could actually be extracted, that the named roles still exist, and that the assumptions about timescales are realistic.

If you are already locked in

If you are planning to leave a supplier whose contract has weak exit terms, the first step is to understand exactly what the contract does and does not require. That shapes the negotiation, the timing of your notice and how much transition support you should budget for.
