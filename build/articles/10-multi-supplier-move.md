Many organisations start with a single managed service provider that runs most of their IT. It is simple to manage: one contract, one account team, one invoice. Over time, though, the arrangement can become expensive and inflexible, and the organisation decides to split the work across several specialist suppliers.

Our first client at Arteze, a UK insurer, made exactly this move. The technology was not the hardest part. The harder questions were about where one supplier's responsibility ends and the next one begins, and who in the client's own team holds it all together.

### Boundaries are where problems appear

With one provider, an incident that crosses several services is still that provider's problem. With several, the same incident can sit between them while each explains why it belongs to someone else. Without clear boundaries, the client ends up coordinating the suppliers itself, often without the people or the processes to do it.

The answer is to define the boundaries in the contracts before the new suppliers start. Each contract should describe not only what that supplier delivers but also how it works with the others: how incidents are handed over, who leads when a problem crosses services, how shared processes such as change and service management will run, and what information each supplier must share.

### Someone has to own the whole

A multi-supplier model needs a function that sees the end-to-end service. In larger organisations this is often called service integration and management, or SIAM. In a mid-sized insurer it may be a small in-house team with clear authority, supported by contract terms that require every supplier to cooperate with it. Whatever it is called, it needs to be designed and staffed before the transition, not after the first major incident.

### Sequencing the move

The order of the transition matters. Moving every service at once concentrates risk. Moving one service at a time takes longer but lets the client learn as it goes. The incumbent's exit obligations also shape the timetable, so it pays to understand those early. We look at exit terms in [a separate article](article-exit-terms.html).

### Regulation adds a further layer

For insurers and banks, a change of this size also brings regulatory expectations. Each new material outsourcing arrangement needs proper due diligence, contract terms that meet the regulator's expectations, and a credible exit plan. Building those requirements into the sourcing process from the start is far easier than adding them later.
