Many supplier disputes do not start with bad faith. They start with a sentence that both sides read differently. "The supplier will provide reasonable support during the transition" sounds clear when the contract is signed. Two years later, the buyer thinks it means a dedicated team for three months, and the supplier thinks it means answering emails when it can.

To avoid this, we test every obligation we draft or review against a simple rule we call W4C. It asks five questions: who, when, what, why, and what is the consequence.

### The five questions

1. **Who** is responsible? Name the party, and where it matters, the role. "The parties will agree" often means nobody acts.
2. **When** must it happen? Give a date, a deadline or a trigger, such as "within ten working days of a request". Without one, there is no point at which the obligation has been missed.
3. **What** exactly must be done or delivered? Describe the output in a way that someone else could check, including the format or standard where relevant.
4. **Why** does it exist? A short statement of purpose helps both sides interpret the obligation sensibly when circumstances change.
5. **What is the consequence** if it is not done? This might be a service credit, a step in the escalation process, a right to withhold payment or, for serious failures, a termination right. An obligation without a consequence is closer to a hope.

### An example

Here is the vague sentence from above, rewritten using the rule.

> The Supplier shall provide a transition team of at least two named engineers for the 12 weeks following the Exit Date, so that the Replacement Supplier can take over the Services without interruption. The team will attend weekly handover meetings and answer written queries within two working days. If the Supplier does not provide this support, the Customer may withhold up to 20 percent of the final quarterly charges until it is provided.

The numbers will vary from contract to contract. What matters is that both sides can now tell whether the obligation has been met.

### Using the rule in your own contracts

You do not need to apply W4C to every line of a contract. Start with the obligations that would hurt most if they failed: service levels, data handling, security, exit and change. Read each one and ask the five questions. Where an answer is missing, that is where a future dispute is most likely to start.
