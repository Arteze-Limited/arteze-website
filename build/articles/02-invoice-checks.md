Most organisations check only a small share of their supplier invoices against the contract. The rest are approved because the total looks close to last month's, the supplier is trusted, and the budget holder is busy. That is understandable, but it means the contract and the invoice slowly drift apart, and nobody notices until someone goes looking.

The drift is rarely dramatic. It tends to come from a handful of ordinary causes. A day rate goes up at the anniversary date, although the contract capped the increase or required notice. A volume discount that should apply above a threshold is never applied, because the supplier's billing system was not set up for it. A milestone payment is invoiced before the milestone has been accepted. A service that was reduced in scope is still billed at the original level. Each one looks small on a single invoice, but over a multi-year contract they add up to real money.

The reason this persists is that accounts payable teams are set up to check that an invoice matches a purchase order, not that it matches the contract. The purchase order often reflects whatever the supplier quoted. So the invoice passes every check it is given, while the terms that were actually negotiated sit in a contract that nobody in the approval chain has read.

### A check you can run this month

Choose your three largest suppliers by annual spend. For each one, take the last six invoices and the current contract, including any change notes. Then ask four questions.

1. Do the rates charged match the rate card in the contract, including any agreed increases?
2. Have any volume discounts, rebates or tiered prices been applied where the volumes qualify?
3. Has every milestone that was invoiced been formally accepted?
4. Does the scope being billed still match the scope you are receiving?

If all four answers are yes for all three suppliers, your controls are in better shape than most. If any answer is no, or nobody can find the information needed to answer, you have found the gap.

### Making it routine

A one-off review recovers money once. The lasting benefit comes from checking invoices against contracts every month, so that errors are caught before they are paid rather than recovered later. That needs three things: a contract register that holds the commercial terms, not just the contract dates; a named person responsible for each major supplier; and a simple monthly record of what was checked, what was found and what was recovered.
