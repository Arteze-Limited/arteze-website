In many organisations, a major renewal starts when the supplier's account manager asks for a meeting. By then the supplier has usually been planning for months. They know the date, the notice period, the price they want and the alternatives you probably do not have time to explore. The buyer starts the conversation already behind.

Automatic renewal makes this worse. Many contracts renew for another term unless notice is given by a fixed date, often 90 or 180 days before expiry. Miss that date and the decision has been made for you, usually with an inflation uplift attached. An automatic renewal is not really a decision. It is what happens when nobody makes one.

### What changes when you start early

The right time to start preparing for a significant renewal is 6 to 12 months before the contract ends. That sounds excessive until you list what needs to happen. You need to understand how the service has actually performed, and whether the scope still matches what the business needs. You need a view of the market, even if you expect to stay with the incumbent. You may need budget approval, a business case, a competitive process or a decision from a board. Each of these takes weeks, and they rarely run in parallel.

Starting early also changes the tone of the negotiation. A supplier who knows you have time to look elsewhere behaves differently from one who knows you do not. You do not have to run a full tender to benefit from this. A credible option is often enough.

### Three dates every contract register should hold

For each significant contract, your register should show three dates, not one.

1. **The expiry date**, which is the one most registers already hold.
2. **The notice date**, which is the last day you can give notice to stop an automatic renewal or to trigger a renegotiation.
3. **The preparation date**, which is when work on the renewal should begin, typically 6 to 12 months before expiry depending on the size and complexity of the contract.

If you only add one thing to your register this quarter, make it the notice date. It is the one that costs the most when it is missed.

### If you have already left it late

With three months to go, you still have options. Check the notice terms first, and give notice if that keeps your choices open. Ask for a short extension on current terms while you review the market. Use whatever performance data you have to anchor the conversation on value rather than price alone.
