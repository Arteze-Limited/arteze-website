Many IT service providers learn that a client is unhappy on the day a tender notice appears. Until then, the monthly service report showed targets met, the service reviews were polite, and the account plan forecast growth. Looking back, the signs were usually there. They just were not in the data the provider was watching.

The problem is that service level agreements measure what was easy to measure when the contract was written. Uptime, ticket response times and resolution targets are useful, but they describe the service from the provider's side. A client executive judges the relationship differently. They ask whether the provider understands their business, whether problems are raised before they become incidents, whether change requests are handled quickly and fairly, and whether the provider brings ideas or just waits to be asked.

A provider can hit every SLA and still fail on all of those. When that happens, the dashboard stays green while the client's view of the relationship slowly turns.

### The signs that usually come first

From the buyer's side of the table, dissatisfaction tends to show itself in quieter ways long before a tender.

- Senior client stakeholders stop attending service reviews and send deputies instead.
- Procurement starts asking for benchmarking data or detailed breakdowns of charges.
- New pieces of work go to other suppliers without much explanation.
- Change requests take longer to approve, or get challenged more often.
- The client's own team starts keeping a separate record of incidents and delays.

None of these proves the account is at risk. Several together usually mean the client has begun to look at its options.

### Why you rarely hear it directly

Clients seldom give their provider candid feedback, because they see little benefit in doing so. Raising concerns can feel like starting a dispute, and if they are already considering a change, they have no reason to warn you. The feedback you do receive is filtered through account teams on both sides who would prefer to keep things calm.

This is why an independent view helps. When a third party with no stake in the outcome interviews client executives in confidence, the answers are usually more direct than anything the provider would hear in a service review.

### What to do about it

Start by comparing two pictures of the same account: what your service data says, and what you believe the client's senior sponsor would say if asked honestly. If you are not sure about the second, that uncertainty is itself useful information.
